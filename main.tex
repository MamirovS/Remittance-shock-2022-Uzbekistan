\begin{center}{\large\bfseries Abstract}\end{center}
\vspace{\baselinedist}
\noindent
Remittances from Russia are the main source of foreign income for millions of households in Uzbekistan, and the February 2022 invasion of Ukraine was one of the largest shocks to that corridor in a generation. Using the Listening to Citizens of Uzbekistan panel, which follows 2,499 households over 82 monthly rounds from September 2018 to June 2025, this thesis asks whether the shock damaged the welfare of the households that depend on it. Compared with households whose migrants worked outside Russia, Russia-exposed households show no measurable deterioration in food security (+0.027, standard error 0.026). That average conceals two opposite experiences. Migrant households are split by employment formality, a status recorded before the invasion and therefore predetermined. In a difference-in-differences design, food insecurity in informal migrant households rose by 27.9 percentage points (standard error 0.042) more than in formal migrant households, and the difference remains visible more than three years later. The two groups track each other over the three pre-shock years from 2019, including the COVID-19 pandemic, when no differential appears. The damage is concentrated in food rather than in committed or health expenditure. The findings suggest that formal employment abroad may function as insurance for the household at home, and that aggregate null results in this literature should be checked for composition before they are read as resilience.
